The person behind the work

Finding possibilities.
Bringing them to life.

I started working at twelve in my mother’s clothing store, steaming, folding, and tagging merchandise. Other jobs followed through high school and college, so retail was part of my life long before it became my career. Later, my sister and I founded and ran a fashion company for a decade alongside my full-time executive roles, working nights and weekends. Those experiences helped shape a conviction I still carry: there is usually a way to get something done, but sometimes you have to look beyond the business or industry you’re in to find it.

I grew up across American, French, and Israeli cultures and hold all three passports. I’ve been traveling since I was a baby; seeing new places and finding new things has always been part of my life. I’m based in Atlanta now, and my working life has taken me through New York, San Francisco, Chicago, Miami, and Fort Worth. Decades of travel across Europe, Asia, and South America have brought lasting relationships and different perspectives. I enjoy the discovery—a place, a product, a conversation, or an approach I haven’t encountered before.

Working with me involves plenty of discussion and brainstorming. My mind moves quickly, and I enjoy exploring ideas with people who bring their own experience and point of view. I want the people working with me to take ownership of their part: to understand what they’re building, make decisions, and feel responsible for the result as if it were their own business. I’m comfortable on a factory floor, in a boardroom, or at a market stall, and I like getting to know the people who make things work.

Cooking brings together much of what I enjoy: creativity, discovery, and people. The combinations are endless, and everyone’s palate is different. Making something new that people will enjoy takes thought and effort, but the smile when they taste it makes that effort worthwhile. I get a similar satisfaction professionally—creating something new, getting it into use, and seeing people respond to it.

Outside work, I paint, make music, and spend time with family and friends. I enjoy golf and pickleball, exploring, and researching whatever has caught my attention. I also like fixing things myself rather than calling a handyman: understanding what’s wrong, learning how it works, and getting it working again. That appetite for finding an answer runs through my work too, whether I’m looking for a supplier, a product, a deal, or something the numbers can tell us. It also draws me to building practical systems with AI and other tools that save time, connect information, and help people get on with their work.

Turning relationships and experience into work that gets done.

I established More Margin Co. as a way to put longstanding relationships and operating experience to work on specific business needs. Suppliers come to me to explore new retail channels; retailers need help with assortments, stores, and the systems behind them. My contribution is connecting the commercial question with what it takes to deliver.

We built financial models to assess the store both on its own and as part of STC’s existing business. The standalone case showed limited profitability, but the projected benefit to the wider business made a stronger case for proceeding.

Retail Development, Private Label & Connected Operations

Connecting the product, the store and the operation

A store-design brief became a connected retail business.

Context

I joined Fun Town RV in November 2021 to develop private-label products and redesign how its stores looked, worked and presented merchandise. The immediate assignment included the new Corsicana location, which opened in March 2022. It was an ambitious starting point: a 65,000-square-foot facility with approximately 10,000 square feet of retail space, a full-service café and restaurant, a service counter and six checkout lanes.

The store brief exposed a wider operating challenge. Locations maintained separate parts catalogs and identifiers, making consolidated reporting and ordering difficult. Retail transactions ran through a system primarily designed for RV dealership operations, products were purchased through distributors, and there was little effective online visibility for the assortment. Improving the stores meant addressing the information, purchasing and fulfillment behind them.

I reported directly to COO Jeff England. Many of the approaches I proposed were new to the company, so explaining how they worked—and why they were worth adopting—was an important part of securing approval. We brought different experiences and processes to the work. Building trust and finding a workable approach together came before greater independence.

  • 65,000 sq ft brought into useCorsicana opened in March 2022, including about 10,000 sq ft of retail.
  • A broader operating briefSeparate location catalogs exposed the need for shared product information and ordering.

The Work

I began connecting the assortment through a shared product database, organizing more than 30,000 SKUs to support more consistent product information, ordering and reporting. Using Shopify and its plugin ecosystem, I developed the retail platform around POS, mobile checkout, the website and marketplace sales. We introduced it at one location before expanding to six across two divisions of the business.

Alongside the systems work, I selected the top 50 selling products as candidates for factory-direct sourcing. I drew on my supplier relationships to develop an assortment under two house brands—KAMPTIME and USA Rec. Supply—connecting product selection, packaging, pricing and store presentation. This gave the dealerships an alternative to buying those products solely through distributors.

The physical stores needed the same consistency. I designed branding, layouts and graphics, developed planograms and merchandising standards, and issued merchandising guides to help teams put the approach into practice. At Corsicana, my design responsibilities extended beyond retail into the RV sales environment, including the sales tower and training rooms.

I also developed the ordering and logistics operation around the products. Inventory moved into third-party logistics, while an internal web portal allowed both dealerships and head office to place store orders and support automatic replenishment. The work connected purchasing and stock availability with how products reached the sales floor and the customer.

  • 30,000+ SKUs unifiedBuilt a shared product database for the retail operation.
  • 2 house brands developedKAMPTIME and USA Rec. Supply.
  • Top 50 products targetedPrioritized high-selling items for factory-direct sourcing.

Outcome

The internal sourcing business reached $3 million in sales in under a year, with Fun Town’s dealerships as its main customers. On the selected factory-direct products, the reported margin increased from 28% to 79%—a 51-percentage-point improvement. That result applied to those products, rather than the margin of the entire business.

The platform expanded from its first location to six across two business divisions. The website and marketplace channels created additional routes to customers, with store inventory also used to fulfill online orders. Product information, retail transactions and ordering were becoming parts of a shared operation rather than separate tasks at each location.

The logistics and internal ordering work reduced errors and overhead and made accounting easier. Just as importantly, the business had practical foundations it could use: its own product offer, a larger retail presence, clearer merchandise standards and new ways to sell and replenish stock.

  • $3M in sourcing salesInternal sales generated in under one year.
  • 28% → 79% product marginReported increase on selected factory-direct products: 51 percentage points.
  • 6 locations, 2 divisionsExpanded the retail platform across the business.

Takeaway

This assignment reinforced that introducing a new way of working requires more than designing the solution. I had to explain unfamiliar capabilities, work through questions and approvals, and adapt my approach to the organization’s pace. I was there to build a business for Fun Town, and the operation needed to make sense to the people who would run it.

The work crossed sourcing, stores, data, sales and logistics. Each part depended on the others, and adoption required clear processes, useful guidance and people who understood their responsibilities. I carried a substantial share of the development myself; the experience strengthened my conviction that building the team and its ability to use the work is part of delivering the business.

  • Built repeatable store standardsCreated merchandising guides and operating processes for teams.
  • Connected sourcing to sellingLinked product records, ordering, logistics and store execution.

Connecting commercial opportunity with an operating model

Finding a different business inside an existing opportunity.

Context

I worked with suppliers and past business partners to find additional sales channels for their products and improve how those products reached stores and customers. I drew on international relationships built over more than twenty years, connecting sourcing and product decisions with sales, logistics and the practical requirements of the businesses involved.

FitBox was a separate venture I created during this period. After leaving Camping World, I continued working with real-estate contacts from the Camping World and Gander project, contributing an operator’s perspective on what could come next for large retail properties.

I kept seeing spaces that were the wrong size for many potential occupants. A smaller business might need 2,000 square feet, while a vacant store offered 35,000. Finding one tenant for the entire building could be difficult—and would leave the property dependent on that business. I saw an opportunity to rethink both the space and the operating model.

  • 20+ years of trading relationshipsApplied an international network to sourcing and new sales channels.
  • Created the FitBox ventureDeveloped a shared-space business model for vacant big-box properties.

The Work

In the consulting work, I connected supplier capabilities with opportunities in other sales channels and examined how products moved into stores and onto shelves. The work brought together sourcing, product development, sales and logistics, with attention to the changes a client’s operation would need to make the commercial opportunity practical.

For FitBox, I developed a model in which we would master-lease a large vacant space and divide it into smaller premises for independent businesses. Fitness, recovery, food and everyday services could serve overlapping customers while retaining their own operations.

The tenant mix and the layout had to work together. Shared reception, lockers, showers and common areas would give smaller operators access to facilities they would otherwise have to build and maintain individually. The aim was to reduce duplicated costs and make the space more accessible to those businesses.

I worked through the relationship between the master rent, smaller tenant leases, common-area charges and the cost of managing shared facilities. The model needed to make commercial sense for the property, the venture and the participating businesses. I also raised capital for the first prototype, contingent on signing a lease.

  • Raised prototype capitalSecured funding for the first FitBox location, contingent on a lease.
  • Built the tenant economicsWorked through master rent, smaller leases and shared-facility costs.

Outcome

The consulting work produced operational recommendations, including projected improvements for a client’s sales and logistics operation. Those projections were separate from FitBox and should not be presented as realized savings.

FitBox progressed from concept development to capital raised for a first prototype, with a lease signing planned for April 2020. When COVID changed the circumstances, we stopped before signing and returned the funds. No investor lost money.

The project did not reach an operating launch. Its proposed benefits—shared infrastructure, smaller commitments for individual businesses and less dependence on a single tenant—remained part of the business model rather than demonstrated operating results.

  • Returned the investors’ capitalStopped before the planned lease signing when COVID changed the conditions.
  • No investor lost moneyFitBox remained a funded concept; it did not open.

Takeaway

My contribution began with an operator’s view of a real-estate problem: what would make this space usable, affordable and practical for the businesses inside it? Answering that meant connecting customer demand, supplier and operator relationships, space planning, shared costs and the economics of the overall venture.

That is the transferable part of this experience. I can identify an opportunity, develop the business model around it and work through what would be required to bring it into use. It also requires judgment about when to proceed. With FitBox, the circumstances changed before the lease commitment, and we stopped and returned the capital.

  • Designed a new use for vacant spaceCombined complementary tenants, smaller premises and shared facilities.
  • Avoided the lease commitmentReassessed the venture before signing and returned the funds.

Cross-department leadership at national scale

Leading a $300M+ project across people, products and stores.

Context

I worked directly with Marcus Lemonis as Camping World expanded across Gander Outdoors, The House, Overton’s, and Specialty Outdoor Retail—a group of independent outdoor retailers with multiple locations. The project exceeded $300 million in scale, and I led hundreds of people across multiple departments. My responsibility combined that cross-department leadership with ownership of the branding, layouts, merchandising, and products that teams needed to deliver.

The Gander reopening program brought particular urgency. Stores ranged from approximately 20,000 to 100,000 square feet, and a consistent brand presentation still had to work within very different buildings. We needed to connect assortments, fixtures, suppliers, and store teams while making disciplined decisions about how much inventory each location could support.

This was a leadership role spanning the connection between the commercial plan and store execution. Across the four businesses, different customers, product categories, channels, and building formats had to be considered together. My work linked the decisions about what we would sell and how we would present it with the practical work required to get stores ready to trade.

  • $300M+ project scopeCross-department leadership on the overall transformation program.
  • Led across 4 businessesCoordinated hundreds of people across departments.

The Work

Leading hundreds of people across departments meant keeping the branding, layout, merchandising, and product work connected. I worked with colleagues, vendors, and operating teams to translate the commercial direction into the details of store execution. I ran point on the initial store sets and developed changes as we discovered what worked on the floor. A plan could look complete on paper and still need substantial adjustment once fixtures, merchandise, and customers occupied the space.

The product program covered RV parts, hunting, fishing, shooting sports, active sports, cycling, apparel, skiing, and convenience, alongside service-related store concepts. I developed private-label identities and assortments around customer needs, price positions, and sales channels, working with suppliers and internal teams to bring them into the stores. In some cases, we manufactured the same item under different names for different channels.

I also developed more than 20 category shop-in-shop concepts to organize these broad assortments into understandable destinations. The work connected the product offer with its presentation: what belonged together, how much space it needed, and how teams would merchandise it.

The scope ran from supplier and product decisions to the space those products would occupy. Layouts affected fixture requirements; category choices affected merchandising; and the assortment had to fit the customer, the channel, and the inventory investment the business could support. I brought those decisions together rather than treating each department’s work as a separate project.

That required both an overall view of the program and close involvement in the initial store sets. I could work through the brand and product direction with Marcus and the teams, then address what needed to change when the plan reached the floor. The aim was a store that people could operate, replenish, and shop—not simply a completed design.

The wider Gander program rebuilt and merchandised more than 80 stores in under 18 months, with teams prepared for opening. My leadership across hundreds of people and multiple departments helped connect the branding, layout, product, and merchandising work within that shared effort. The scale of the rollout depended on teams executing together across the program.

As stores opened, a different commercial opportunity became clear. The smaller formats were compact and effective, but filling the larger buildings required more merchandise than the budget could reasonably support. I proposed carving out space for RV sales inside those buildings. The company adopted that approach, putting more of the footprint behind its core business instead of filling every available area with retail inventory.

We then reconsidered smaller underperforming locations. Rather than buying out their leases, we developed the express model: a smaller dealership supported by an extensive logistics system for delivering RVs. My contribution was identifying how the format needed to change and connecting the space, merchandise requirements, and operating model.

My contribution was connecting those decisions—from products and supplier relationships to layouts, merchandising, and execution with teams. Leading at this scale also reinforced the connection between people and commercial decisions. A change in assortment or floor space could affect several departments at once. My role was to keep those decisions connected across the teams while staying close enough to the work to recognize when the original approach needed to change.

Turning the plan into something that works.

  1. Identify the opportunity

    • I start with the customer, the need and what is already available—looking for a useful gap in the offer or a new route to market.
    • I draw on the team’s knowledge, sales information and supplier relationships to understand where the business has a reason to compete.

  2. Make the numbers work

Understand the work first

Define the operating need before choosing the tools.

  • I start with who needs to do what, what information they need, and where the next person gets held up.
  • I trace the handoffs between stores, purchasing, online sales and fulfillment, looking for duplicate entry, disconnected records and unclear responsibilities.
  • At Fun Town RV, separate location catalogs and inconsistent product identifiers helped define the need for shared information before I selected and connected the tools.

Why it matters: The operating need determines the system, so the tools support how the business actually works.

Give product information a common structure

Give purchasing, selling and reporting a shared foundation.

  • At Fun Town RV, I organized more than 30,000 stock-keeping units—individual product records—into a shared database for ordering and reporting.
  • I connected product information with image files and the pricing matrix, giving the related applications and stores a consistent foundation.
  • We used Google Workspace, mainly Sheets, for data input, output and dashboards. That gave the team a familiar way to work with the information supporting the operation.

Why it matters: Consistent records help purchasing, selling and reporting refer to the same products and pricing information.

Connect stores, orders and fulfillment

Connect daily transactions across locations and channels.

  • I designed and initially implemented the connections between the product databases, supporting applications and Shopify stores.
  • The broader retail setup brought together point of sale, online channels, internal ordering and fulfillment. It started at one location and expanded to six locations across two divisions.
  • Much of the ongoing operation ran automatically. Dashboard reviews and health checks remained part of keeping it working.

Why it matters: Connected information supports execution across locations and channels, with oversight when something needs attention.

Make purchasing and replenishment actionable

Connect the buying decision with availability and delivery.

  • I developed an internal ordering portal for dealerships and head office, alongside automatic replenishment and third-party fulfillment arrangements.
  • I organize product, supplier, cost and pricing information around the decisions people need to make: what to order, when it is needed and how it will reach the next location or customer.
  • For imported products, I include import costs when assessing the economics, so purchasing decisions reflect more than the factory price.

Why it matters: Ordering and replenishment become part of an operating process that supports availability and margin discipline.

Make performance and system health visible

Use information to decide where attention is needed.

  • We used Google Sheets for dashboards and data flowing into and out of the operation.
  • I used dashboards and health checks to oversee a system that was largely running on its own, identifying where attention was needed.
  • My broader reporting work has followed the same principle: make information useful for a decision, then connect that decision to action and follow-through.

Why it matters: Visibility helps people direct their attention and decide what to do next.

Make the work repeatable

Give people a way of working they can maintain and improve.

  • I handled the architecture, plan and initial implementation, then delegated routine work to a small team.
  • Alongside the systems, I developed layouts, planograms and merchandising guides to help stores put the approach into practice consistently.
  • I connect the tools with instructions, responsibilities and team feedback, so the operation can keep improving as people use it.

Why it matters: The organization gains a way of working that people can understand, maintain and repeat.
