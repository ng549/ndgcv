The person behind the work

Curious about people.
Always figuring something out.

I started working at twelve in my mother’s clothing store, steaming, folding, and tagging merchandise. Other jobs followed through high school and college. Retail was part of my life long before it became my career.

I grew up across three cultures and hold three passports: American, French, and Israeli. I’m based in Atlanta now, and my working life has taken me through New York, San Francisco, Chicago, Miami, and Fort Worth. Decades of travel across Europe, Asia, and South America have brought relationships, different perspectives, and a lot of questions I would never have thought to ask at home.

I like people. I bring energy to a conversation, enjoy getting to know how someone thinks, and am comfortable moving between a factory floor, a boardroom, and a market stall. I like finding things—a supplier, a product, a deal, or an answer buried in the numbers. If I don’t understand something, I tend to stay with it until I do.

For a decade, I founded and ran a fashion company with my sister alongside full-time executive roles, working nights and weekends. That same curiosity now takes me into systems and tools built with AI: finding practical ways to save time, connect information, and help people get on with their work.

Outside work, I cook, make art, and enjoy time with family and friends. Golf and pickleball bring out my competitive side. Exploring and researching satisfy the curious side. And there is usually something to fix: I never hire a handyman because I enjoy figuring out what’s wrong, learning how it works, and getting it working again.

The starting point

I was working with past partners to find new sales channels for their products when another opportunity kept catching my attention: empty big-box stores. A 35,000 sq ft space was too large for a boutique needing 2,000 sq ft, while a single large tenant left the owner dependent on one business. I wanted to work out a different use for the space.

02The work
  • That became the FitBox concept: lease the larger space and divide it into smaller shops, with independent businesses sharing facilities such as reception, lockers, and showers.
  • The idea was to lower the cost of opening for each operator and bring businesses with overlapping customers together.
  • Rent, common-area charges, and the fee for managing shared facilities all had to support the larger lease.
  • The shared facilities meant each operator did not have to build and pay for the same amenities separately.
  • The master lease, smaller leases, and common-area fees had to work together financially.
03What changed
  • The model spread the dependence across different businesses, so one tenant leaving would not empty the entire property.
  • Alongside that work, I restructured a key client’s sales and logistics operations, improving labor efficiency and cash flow by roughly $1M per year.
  • The mix mattered: fitness, recovery, food, and everyday services could bring different customers and different patterns of demand.
  • The property would not depend on one tenant, franchise, or fitness trend.
04What it led to
  • What interested me was making an oversized space useful to smaller businesses.
  • A vacancy could become a 2,000 sq ft problem rather than a 35,000 sq ft one.
  • Working through that idea meant paying attention to both the property economics and how the businesses would operate together.

Retail Operations

I look at what happens in the store each day, listen to the people doing the work, and check the numbers. Then I work with the team on changes they can understand and use. Performance measures and customer feedback help me decide what needs attention. Clear daily routines give the people on the floor a way to put those decisions into practice, and tell us whether the changes are helping.

Multi-Unit Strategy

Across several locations, I look for what should be consistent and what needs to stay local. Training, clear communication, and shared reporting help store teams put those decisions into practice. The detail is in the rollout: shared merchandising standards, useful performance comparisons, and ongoing training. I want each manager to understand the common approach and have room to respond to the customers in that location.

Operational Turnaround

I start with where the business is losing margin and where work gets stuck. I work with the team on a few clear priorities, simpler routines, and tighter financial control. That means understanding the reasons behind weak performance before changing the routine. Frontline knowledge matters: changes need to make sense to the people expected to carry them out, with clear communication and a way to track progress.

Space Management

I use product performance to plan floor space, fixtures, and product placement. The aim is to make the store easier to shop and make better use of the space. I look at sales per square foot, planograms, and how customers move through the store. A layout has to give important products the right exposure while remaining practical for the team to merchandise and replenish.

Inventory Control

I look at what sells, what sits, and where stock is running short. I work on purchasing, replenishment, and stock routines so teams can serve demand without tying up more cash than needed. Sales trends guide the buying, but the daily routines matter too: accurate tracking, clear stock procedures, and attention to shrink. I want fewer missed sales and less money sitting in products that are not moving.

Sourcing

I work with domestic and international suppliers on product, cost, quality, and delivery. Clear requirements and lasting relationships matter when the product has to arrive as agreed. I pay attention to supplier vetting, clear standards, quality control, and delivery commitments. Those conversations are easier when the relationship has been built over time and both sides know what is expected.

Private Label

I look for gaps in the assortment where an owned brand can offer the customer a useful choice and improve margin. The work runs from product and sourcing through packaging and placement in the store. The range needs a clear place beside the national brands, with quality that earns repeat purchases. I work through the product, factory, packaging, and shelf position together because a better buying price alone does not create a brand.

Product Development

I work through what the customer needs, how the product fits the brand, and what it must cost. That includes testing, design changes, and working with manufacturers on production and timing. Samples and revisions are part of the work, not an afterthought. I stay close to the manufacturer on quality, timing, and cost so the finished product reflects the original brief.

Brand Development

I connect the product, packaging, and marketing around a clear reason for the customer to choose the brand. My work includes new concepts and updates to existing brands. The same promise needs to come through on the product, the package, the website, and the retail floor. I work through those details so a customer can understand what the brand stands for and why it belongs in their basket.

Assortment Strategy

I look at what customers buy, what earns margin, and what no longer belongs in the range. Then I work through what to keep, add, or remove. I weigh dependable sellers against margin opportunities and changing customer demand. Category performance, competitors, and the customer base help guide that balance; the assortment needs regular editing as the business changes.

Margin Improvement

I look across product costs, pricing, supplier terms, sales mix, and stock turnover. The work is to find where margin is being lost and make practical changes without compromising quality. A lower unit cost is only part of the answer. I also look at waste, operating costs, and the cash tied up in slow stock, then work with the teams responsible for pricing, purchasing, and replenishment.

Pricing Strategy

I compare costs, competitor prices, customer demand, and stock levels. Then I work through price tiers and promotions, with regular reviews as those conditions change. The decision needs to account for where the item sits in the range and what a promotion will do to margin. I keep reviewing the relationship between price, demand, and inventory rather than treating the first price as permanent.

Vendor Negotiation

I prepare around what the business needs and what the supplier can deliver. The conversation includes price, shipping, returns, and marketing support, with the longer relationship in mind. I go into the discussion knowing the terms that matter beyond the invoice price. A useful agreement has to work through delivery, returns, and support as well as cost, and leave both sides able to do business again.

E-Commerce

I connect the online store with inventory, orders, and fulfillment. I use sales and customer data to work through how the site and its marketing can improve. The sale is only one step: stock accuracy and fulfillment have to hold up behind it. I look at the customer experience and channel performance alongside the operating work needed to get the order out.

Acquisition Integration

I look at how both businesses work and what each does well. I work with the teams to keep useful knowledge, agree on shared routines, and continue serving customers through the change. Keeping experienced people and their knowledge is part of the integration. I look for the best practices in both businesses, make the decisions clear, and work through the transition without losing sight of the customer.

Portfolio Optimization

I assess the performance and potential of each business or location. That helps frame decisions about where to invest, where a turnaround is needed, and where to consider an exit. How a business problem becomes a working tool

Ten steps, with checks along the way. The people who will use the system are part of deciding whether it works.

  1. 01

    Define the business problem

    Talk through where time is lost, what people need, and what would make the work easier.

    Leave with: A problem worth solving
  2. 02

    Research what already exists

    Look at existing tools, previous work, and the constraints before deciding what needs to be built.

    Leave with: A reuse, connect, or build decision
  3. 03

    Agree what the tool must do

    Write down the users, the steps, the screens, and what a useful result looks like.

    Leave with: A clear brief
  4. 04

    Map the system

    Work through how information moves, which tools connect, and where people make decisions.

    Leave with: A system map
  5. 05

    Give each AI a clear job

    Use separate agents for defined tasks, with shared instructions and clear handoffs.

    Leave with: Work that can move in parallel
  6. 06

    Keep the work in one place

    Save the code, decisions, and handoffs in GitHub so the next person can follow what happened.

    Leave with: A shared record
  7. 07

    Build and connect in small pieces

    Put one useful part in place, check it, and connect it to the next.

    Leave with: A working version
  8. 08

    Test the actual work

    Check the numbers, the steps, and what happens when something goes wrong.

    Leave with: Evidence it behaves as intended
  9. 09

    Review before release

    I review the important decisions and the result before it goes into use.

    Leave with: A reviewed release
  10. 10

    Use it and improve it

    Learn from the people using it, remove friction, and carry that learning into the next version.

    Leave with: A better next version

Adaptive Audio Engine

ATOMIQ

LIVE 1.0 · ADAPTIVE AUDIO ENGINE
  • Started as a real DJ app running in the browser — four decks, real audio, MIDI hardware support, track analysis, and saved libraries — and grew into ATOMIQ LIVE 1.0, a complete performance system.
  • The Adaptive Audio Engine is the system's shared clock: decks, waveforms, stems, hardware lights, and visuals all stay in time with the music's phrase, key, and energy. The software spots the moment; the DJ makes the call.
  • Tested like serious software: hardware control passed 400/400 checks three runs in a row, the interface 489/489, and the intelligence layer 433/433 — with mix scoring proven against a 5,000-track library.
  • The intelligence picks the best next track, explains exactly how to mix into it, and can even mix on its own — but the moment the DJ touches a fader, control snaps back instantly. It helps the performer, never replaces them.
  • The product site, the live browser booth (desktop and phone), hardware connections, video output for the crowd, and one unified music library were all built with AI as the tools (Grok + Claude) — directed like a product team, not generated as a mockup.
