About me

A little about me.

I started working at twelve in my mother’s clothing store, steaming, folding, and tagging merchandise. Other jobs followed through high school and college. Retail was part of my life long before it became my career.

I’m American, French, and Israeli, based in Atlanta. I grew up across three cultures and have spent decades traveling through Europe, Asia, and South America. I like finding things—a supplier, a product, a deal, or an answer buried in the numbers—and I tend to stay with a question until I understand it.

For a decade, I founded and ran a fashion company alongside full-time executive roles, working nights and weekends. These days, that curiosity also takes me into building systems and tools with AI—ways to save time, reduce repetitive work, and make information easier to use.

Outside work, there’s family, cooking, and usually something to fix. I never hire a handyman—I enjoy figuring out what’s wrong, learning how it works, and getting it working again.

From a gap on the shelf
to a product in hand.

Each brand started with a question about the assortment: what was missing, and could we offer something better? The answer had to work all the way from product and packaging through sourcing, delivery, and the sale.

Follow the product from idea to sale

Read down the steps. Each decision gives the next team something concrete to work with.

  1. 01

    Find the gap

    Category where a national brand takes the margin. Define customer, price tier, and the brand it has to beat.

    Leave with: A clear customer, price point, and gap to fill
  2. 02

    Build the brand

    Name, logo suite, color standards, typography, packaging, hangtags, and usage rules.

    Leave with: A brand identity and packaging direction
  3. 03

    Develop the product

    Specs, materials, and features written on our side. Samples, testing, and revisions until it beats the incumbent.

    Leave with: An agreed specification and tested sample
  4. 04

    Source and negotiate

    Factory-direct through a twenty-year vendor network. Cost, tooling, minimums, and terms settled before commitment.

    Leave with: A supplier, cost, minimum order, and terms
  5. 05

    Import

    Freight, customs, compliance, and labeling. Landed cost modeled before a unit ships.

    Leave with: A landed cost and shipment plan
  6. 06

    Warehouse and allocate

    Receive, allocate, and replenish across distribution centers and a national store fleet.

    Leave with: Stock in the right locations and a replenishment plan
  7. 07

    Sell four ways

    Own site, marketplaces, wholesale, and the retail floor — four ways to sell the same assortment.

    Leave with: A connected offer across four sales channels

Start with what the customer needs

Every brand began the same way: a category where we were selling somebody else's label at somebody else's margin. Nepallo came out of fishing and sun protection, aimed squarely at Columbia PFG and Huk. Outfitta came out of active apparel. Before a single sample existed there was a customer, a price tier, a competitor, and a reason to exist — then the identity, the color standards, the hangtag, and the fabric story that had to survive being held in a customer's hand.

Nepallo · Outfitta · Ultimate Terrain

Work through the product

An owned brand needs to give the customer a reason to choose it, through the product, the price, or both. Temp360 was the clearest case: rechargeable heated base layers, socks, and glove liners, built around a technology story a customer could understand in three seconds on a shelf. That meant specs written on our side, factory samples, testing, and packaging that sold the feature before the price.

Temp360 — heated apparel

Get it from the factory to the store

The work continued well beyond design. Each line was sourced factory-direct through a vendor network built over twenty years, negotiated, imported, cleared, labeled, warehoused, and allocated across a national fleet of stores. Landed cost was modeled before a single unit shipped, because the margin only exists if the freight math works.

Grill World · Tow Center of America · camping hardlines

Apply the thinking across categories

The portfolio kept widening. Marine brought Dockmate for docking hardware, lighting and ladders, Covermate for boat and pontoon covers, a pontoon furniture and hardware line, and Gladiator for towables and watersports. Fishing ran from value combos up to an angler tier of rods and reels. Hunting added value and best-tier fieldwear, Trophy Boss for deer gear, waterfowl and archery labels, and two tactical brands for the shooting customer. Flex came in from the other direction — a West Coast watch and watersports lifestyle brand. Each one needed its own identity, tiering, sourcing, and shelf logic.

Dockmate · Covermate · Gladiator · Trophy Boss · Flex

Make it work in each sales channel

Each brand ran its own site, its own marketplace listings, wholesale accounts, and the retail floor — one assortment with different pricing rules and content needs in each channel. Margin on private label ran far above national brands, and the owned brands gave customers a range they could not buy elsewhere.

2019—21Big Box GroupBig Box Group worked with retail, e-commerce, and consumer-product businesses on sourcing, product development, sales, logistics, and operations. Its work connected international suppliers with the needs of retailers and their customers.Partner / Consultant · Miami, FL

Miami-based retail consultancy reimagining vacant big-box real estate for owners and lease holders.

Where I started
  • I was working with past partners to find new sales channels for their products when another opportunity kept catching my attention: empty big-box stores.
  • A 35,000 sq ft space was too large for a boutique needing 2,000 sq ft, while a single large tenant left the owner dependent on one business.
  • I wanted to work out a different use for the space.
What I worked on
  • That became the FitBox concept: lease the larger space and divide it into smaller shops, with independent businesses sharing facilities such as reception, lockers, and showers.
  • The idea was to lower the cost of opening for each operator and bring businesses with overlapping customers together.
  • Rent, common-area charges, and the fee for managing shared facilities all had to support the larger lease.
What came of it
  • The model spread the dependence across different businesses, so one tenant leaving would not empty the entire property.
  • Alongside that work, I restructured a key client’s sales and logistics operations, improving labor efficiency and cash flow by roughly $1M per year.
What stayed with me
  • What interested me was making an oversized space useful to smaller businesses.
  • A vacancy could become a 2,000 sq ft problem rather than a 35,000 sq ft one.
  • Working through that idea meant paying attention to both the property economics and how the businesses would operate together.

Systems, tools & AI

Less time chasing.
More time doing.

I build systems and tools that make the work easier, faster, and more connected: ordering, product information, reporting, and the steps between people.

It starts with the business and the people doing the work. I use AI to research, design, build, and check possible answers. Software is one part of that; the point is a useful tool the organization can actually use.

How a business problem becomes a working tool

Ten steps, with checks along the way. The people who will use the system are part of deciding whether it works.

  1. 01

    Define the business problem

    Talk through where time is lost, what people need, and what would make the work easier.

    Leave with: A problem worth solving
  2. 02

    Research what already exists

    Look at existing tools, previous work, and the constraints before deciding what needs to be built.

    Leave with: A reuse, connect, or build decision
  3. 03

    Agree what the tool must do

    Write down the users, the steps, the screens, and what a useful result looks like.

    Leave with: A clear brief
  4. 04

    Map the system

    Work through how information moves, which tools connect, and where people make decisions.

    Leave with: A system map
  5. 05

    Give each AI a clear job

    Use separate agents for defined tasks, with shared instructions and clear handoffs.

    Leave with: Work that can move in parallel
  6. 06

    Keep the work in one place

    Save the code, decisions, and handoffs in GitHub so the next person can follow what happened.

    Leave with: A shared record
  7. 07

    Build and connect in small pieces

    Put one useful part in place, check it, and connect it to the next.

    Leave with: A working version
  8. 08

    Test the actual work

    Check the numbers, the steps, and what happens when something goes wrong.

    Leave with: Evidence it behaves as intended
  9. 09

    Review before release

    I review the important decisions and the result before it goes into use.

    Leave with: A reviewed release
  10. 10

    Use it and improve it

    Learn from the people using it, remove friction, and carry that learning into the next version.

    Leave with: A better next version

↺ Use it, learn from the team, and feed the next problem back into the process.

Adaptive Audio Engine

ATOMIQ

LIVE 1.0 · ADAPTIVE AUDIO ENGINE
  • Started as a real DJ app running in the browser — four decks, real audio, MIDI hardware support, track analysis, and saved libraries — and grew into ATOMIQ LIVE 1.0, a complete performance system.
  • The Adaptive Audio Engine is the system's shared clock: decks, waveforms, stems, hardware lights, and visuals all stay in time with the music's phrase, key, and energy. The software spots the moment; the DJ makes the call.
  • Tested like serious software: hardware control passed 400/400 checks three runs in a row, the interface 489/489, and the intelligence layer 433/433 — with mix scoring proven against a 5,000-track library.
  • The intelligence picks the best next track, explains exactly how to mix into it, and can even mix on its own — but the moment the DJ touches a fader, control snaps back instantly. It helps the performer, never replaces them.
  • The product site, the live browser booth (desktop and phone), hardware connections, video output for the crowd, and one unified music library were all built with AI as the tools (Grok + Claude) — directed like a product team, not generated as a mockup.
How it comes together
  1. 01

    Business problem

  2. 02

  3. 03

  4. 04

    Architecture

    Designed the Adaptive Audio Engine as one shared clock for every surface

  5. 05

    Parallel AI agents

    Grok and Claude built decks, mixer, library, and visuals side by side

  6. 06

    GitHub — source of truth

    Every module, test log, and handoff lived in one repo

  7. 07

    Build & integration

  8. 08

  9. 09
