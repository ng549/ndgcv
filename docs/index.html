Retail Development, Private Label & Connected Operations

Connecting the product, the store and the operation

A store-design brief became a connected retail business.

Context

I joined Fun Town RV in November 2021 to develop private-label products and redesign how its stores looked, worked and presented merchandise. The immediate assignment included the new Corsicana location, which opened in March 2022. It was an ambitious starting point: a 65,000-square-foot facility with approximately 10,000 square feet of retail space, a full-service café and restaurant, a service counter and six checkout lanes.

The store brief exposed a wider operating challenge. Locations maintained separate parts catalogs and identifiers, making consolidated reporting and ordering difficult. Retail transactions ran through a system primarily designed for RV dealership operations, products were purchased through distributors, and there was little effective online visibility for the assortment. Improving the stores meant addressing the information, purchasing and fulfillment behind them.

I reported directly to COO Jeff England. Many of the approaches I proposed were new to the company, so explaining how they worked—and why they were worth adopting—was an important part of securing approval. We brought different experiences and processes to the work. Building trust and finding a workable approach together came before greater independence.

  • 65,000 sq ft brought into useCorsicana opened in March 2022, including about 10,000 sq ft of retail.
  • 6 checkout lanes establishedRetail, café and service functions in one facility.

The Work

I began connecting the assortment through a shared product database, organizing more than 30,000 SKUs to support more consistent product information, ordering and reporting. Using Shopify and its plugin ecosystem, I developed the retail platform around POS, mobile checkout, the website and marketplace sales. We introduced it at one location before expanding to six across two divisions of the business.

Alongside the systems work, I selected the top 50 selling products as candidates for factory-direct sourcing. I drew on my supplier relationships to develop an assortment under two house brands—KAMPTIME and USA Rec. Supply—connecting product selection, packaging, pricing and store presentation. This gave the dealerships an alternative to buying those products solely through distributors.

The physical stores needed the same consistency. I designed branding, layouts and graphics, developed planograms and merchandising standards, and issued merchandising guides to help teams put the approach into practice. At Corsicana, my design responsibilities extended beyond retail into the RV sales environment, including the sales tower and training rooms.

I also developed the ordering and logistics operation around the products. Inventory moved into third-party logistics, while an internal web portal allowed both dealerships and head office to place store orders and support automatic replenishment. The work connected purchasing and stock availability with how products reached the sales floor and the customer.

  • 30,000+ SKUs unifiedBuilt a shared product database for the retail operation.
  • 2 house brands developedKAMPTIME and USA Rec. Supply.
  • Top 50 products targetedPrioritized high-selling items for factory-direct sourcing.

Outcome

The internal sourcing business reached $3 million in sales in under a year, with Fun Town’s dealerships as its main customers. On the selected factory-direct products, the reported margin increased from 28% to 79%—a 51-percentage-point improvement. That result applied to those products, rather than the margin of the entire business.

The platform expanded from its first location to six across two business divisions. The website and marketplace channels created additional routes to customers, with store inventory also used to fulfill online orders. Product information, retail transactions and ordering were becoming parts of a shared operation rather than separate tasks at each location.

The logistics and internal ordering work reduced errors and overhead and made accounting easier. Just as importantly, the business had practical foundations it could use: its own product offer, a larger retail presence, clearer merchandise standards and new ways to sell and replenish stock.

I had to explain unfamiliar capabilities, work through questions and approvals, and adapt my approach to the organization’s pace. I was there to build a business for Fun Town, and the operation needed to make sense to the people who would run it.

Connecting commercial opportunity with an operating model

Finding a different business inside an existing opportunity.

Context

I worked with suppliers and past business partners to find additional sales channels for their products and improve how those products reached stores and customers. I drew on international relationships built over more than twenty years, connecting sourcing and product decisions with sales, logistics and the practical requirements of the businesses involved.

FitBox was a separate venture I created during this period. After leaving Camping World, I continued working with real-estate contacts from the Camping World and Gander project, contributing an operator’s perspective on what could come next for large retail properties.

I kept seeing spaces that were the wrong size for many potential occupants. A smaller business might need 2,000 square feet, while a vacant store offered 35,000. Finding one tenant for the entire building could be difficult—and would leave the property dependent on that business. I saw an opportunity to rethink both the space and the operating model.

  • 20+ years of trading relationshipsApplied an international network to sourcing and new sales channels.
  • Created the FitBox ventureDeveloped a shared-space business model for vacant big-box properties.

The Work

In the consulting work, I connected supplier capabilities with opportunities in other sales channels and examined how products moved into stores and onto shelves. The work brought together sourcing, product development, sales and logistics, with attention to the changes a client’s operation would need to make the commercial opportunity practical.

For FitBox, I developed a model in which we would master-lease a large vacant space and divide it into smaller premises for independent businesses. Fitness, recovery, food and everyday services could serve overlapping customers while retaining their own operations.

The tenant mix and the layout had to work together. Shared reception, lockers, showers and common areas would give smaller operators access to facilities they would otherwise have to build and maintain individually. The aim was to reduce duplicated costs and make the space more accessible to those businesses.

I worked through the relationship between the master rent, smaller tenant leases, common-area charges and the cost of managing shared facilities. The model needed to make commercial sense for the property, the venture and the participating businesses. I also raised capital for the first prototype, contingent on signing a lease.

  • Raised prototype capitalSecured funding for the first FitBox location, contingent on a lease.
  • Built the tenant economicsWorked through master rent, smaller leases and shared-facility costs.

Outcome

The consulting work produced operational recommendations, including projected improvements for a client’s sales and logistics operation. Those projections were separate from FitBox and should not be presented as realized savings.

FitBox progressed from concept development to capital raised for a first prototype, with a lease signing planned for April 2020. When COVID changed the circumstances, we stopped before signing and returned the funds. No investor lost money.

The project did not reach an operating launch. Its proposed benefits—shared infrastructure, smaller commitments for individual businesses and less dependence on a single tenant—remained part of the business model rather than demonstrated operating results.

  • Returned the investors’ capitalStopped before the planned lease signing when COVID changed the conditions.
  • No investor lost moneyFitBox remained a funded concept; it did not open.

Takeaway

My contribution began with an operator’s view of a real-estate problem: what would make this space usable, affordable and practical for the businesses inside it? Answering that meant connecting customer demand, supplier and operator relationships, space planning, shared costs and the economics of the overall venture.

That is the transferable part of this experience. I can identify an opportunity, develop the business model around it and work through what would be required to bring it into use. It also requires judgment about when to proceed. With FitBox, the circumstances changed before the lease commitment, and we stopped and returned the capital.

Building the business, from the product to the store.

Context

My sister and I started COURAGE. b in 2008, building on what we had learned growing up around our mother’s clothing business. We wanted to create our own offer and source products directly, giving us greater control over the merchandise and its margin.

My beginning in Scarsdale was a folding table and a steamer. Established brands did not want to sell to us yet, so we had to build an offer of our own. My mother designed the items, my sister ran the stores, and I funded the business and built the foundations that allowed it to operate.

I opened the stores and physically built them with my own hands. My responsibilities extended from leases and manufacturing to logistics, tagging, and pricing. I continued building the business alongside other executive roles, with each of us contributing a different part of the family’s experience.

  • Co-founded and funded COURAGE. bStarted the business with my sister in 2008.
  • Built the stores myselfHandled leases, financing and the physical work of opening locations.

The Work

I initially handled some of our manufacturing and eventually took responsibility for all of it. We developed production relationships across France, Italy, China, India, Vietnam, New York, and Los Angeles. My mother’s designs had to become products we could manufacture, price, move, and put into stores.

Approximately 90% of our merchandise was private label in the early business. Direct sourcing gave us more control over product costs and pricing, but it also meant committing money to production and carrying the resulting inventory. The manufacturing decision and the selling decision were connected.

Store development brought another set of commitments. During the financial crisis, I saw an opportunity to negotiate favorable leases in strong markets. I handled leases, opened locations, and did the physical work of building stores, alongside the less visible work of getting merchandise delivered, tagged, and priced.

Because I was funding the business, these decisions had immediate financial consequences. A production order, a lease, a selling price, and the stock left at the end of a season all affected the same business. My work connected those decisions with the stores my sister operated and the products my mother designed.

Outcome

We expanded into markets including New York City, Scarsdale, Greenwich, Englewood, East Hampton, Palm Beach, Aspen, and Bethesda, alongside outlet locations in Fairfax and New York City. We later sold a portion of the business to ML Fashion. Acquisitions and further openings followed as we helped build ML Fashion Group. This was a change in ownership and a new stage of expansion.

    I start with the customer, the assortment and the economics. A product needs a reason to exist before it needs a supplier.

    How I work through it

    Compare the current range, customer needs, price tiers and national-brand benchmarks. Separate a genuine gap from another variation of something we already sell. Establish the target selling price and the margin the business needs.

    The handoff

    Merchandising and finance agree the customer, target price, initial volume and margin objective.

    What the organization gains

    The organization puts development time and working capital behind a defined opportunity, with a shared test for whether the idea is worth pursuing.

  2. 02Give the brand a jobThe brand should help a customer understand why this product belongs in their life and why it belongs in our assortment.+

    The brand should help a customer understand why this product belongs in their life and why it belongs in our assortment.

    How I work through it

    Define the position and price tier, then develop the name, visual identity, packaging, hangtags and usage standards together. Make the differentiation visible at shelf distance and understandable on a small product page.

    The handoff

    A brand brief, identity standards and packaging direction that design, buying and marketing can all use.

    What the organization gains

    The company owns a recognizable proposition. Teams present one coherent offer across categories and channels instead of creating disconnected products.

  3. 03Turn the brief into a tested productI translate the commercial brief into specifications a factory can make and a buyer can judge.+

    I translate the commercial brief into specifications a factory can make and a buyer can judge.

    How I work through it

    Write down materials, dimensions, features and packaging requirements. Review samples against the brief and the benchmark product. Record revisions, test the next sample and resolve differences before approving production.

    The handoff

    An agreed specification and approved sample. If the sample fails the brief, it returns for revision before the order moves forward.

    What the organization gains

    Buying, quality and the supplier work from the same definition of acceptable. Catching a problem on the sample table is much less disruptive than discovering it after arrival.

  4. 04Choose the factory and settle the termsMy supplier relationships help open the conversation; the quality and commercial requirements determine the agreement.+

    My supplier relationships help open the conversation; the quality and commercial requirements determine the agreement.

    How I work through it

    Compare capability, cost, minimum quantities, tooling, lead times and payment terms. Work through trade-offs with the buying team. Keep the agreed sample, specification and order terms connected so that a lower quote does not quietly become a different product.

    The handoff

    A supplier agreement with clear product standards, quantities, costs, timing and responsibilities.

    What the organization gains

    The organization can make an informed commitment. It understands the cash requirement and delivery assumptions before placing the order.

  5. 05Plan the landed cost and arrivalA factory price is only one part of what the business will pay. I work through the route to our receiving dock before the product ships.+

    A factory price is only one part of what the business will pay. I work through the route to our receiving dock before the product ships.

    How I work through it

    Bring freight, customs, labeling and applicable compliance requirements into the plan. Connect shipment timing with launch timing and receiving capacity. Revisit the selling economics using landed cost, rather than leaving those costs for someone else to discover later.

    The handoff

    A landed-cost model, shipment plan and receiving requirements shared with finance and operations.

    What the organization gains

    Finance sees the real economics, operations can prepare, and the launch plan reflects when stock can actually become available.

  6. 06Make the inventory usefulThe handoff from a shipment to saleable stock needs as much attention as the initial product idea.+

    The handoff from a shipment to saleable stock needs as much attention as the initial product idea.

    How I work through it

    Set up product records, receiving, allocation and replenishment across the distribution center and store fleet. Match the initial allocation to the selling plan, then use demand to adjust where stock goes. Make ownership of exceptions clear.

    The handoff

    Accurate item records, available inventory and an allocation and replenishment plan the team can run.

    What the organization gains

    The right teams can find and sell the stock. This reduces avoidable handoffs and helps keep capital from sitting in the wrong location.

  7. 07Launch, listen and improveI connect the product to four routes to the customer: our own website, marketplaces, wholesale and stores.+

    I connect the product to four routes to the customer: our own website, marketplaces, wholesale and stores.

    How I work through it

    Align product information, pricing, packaging and presentation across channels. Give selling teams the information they need. Review sales, margin, availability and customer feedback together, then feed what we learn into the next buy or product revision.

    The handoff

    A coordinated offer, a performance review and a decision to replenish, revise or retire the product.

    What the organization gains

    The business gains an owned assortment it can keep improving. The sale becomes information for the next decision, closing the loop from customer need to customer response.

ATOMIQ

LIVE 1.0 · ADAPTIVE AUDIO ENGINE
The idea behind the work

Music is another place where I like figuring out how the pieces connect. ATOMIQ began as a browser-based DJ application and grew into a performance system organized around one shared musical clock.

The challenge is to keep audio, hardware and visuals working together while leaving the performance decisions with the DJ. It is an example of how I map a complicated live workflow into connected tools with a clear human operator.

Inside the work
  • Started as a real DJ app running in the browser — four decks, real audio, MIDI hardware support, track analysis, and saved libraries — and grew into ATOMIQ LIVE 1.0, a complete performance system.
  • The Adaptive Audio Engine is the system's shared clock: decks, waveforms, stems, hardware lights, and visuals all stay in time with the music's phrase, key, and energy. The software spots the moment; the DJ makes the call.
  • Tested like serious software: hardware control passed 400/400 checks three runs in a row, the interface 489/489, and the intelligence layer 433/433 — with mix scoring proven against a 5,000-track library.
  • The intelligence picks the best next track, explains exactly how to mix into it, and can even mix on its own — but the moment the DJ touches a fader, control snaps back instantly. It helps the performer, never replaces them.
  • The product site, the live browser booth (desktop and phone), hardware connections, video output for the crowd, and one unified music library were all built with AI as the tools (Grok + Claude) — directed like a product team, not generated as a mockup.

My interest in pickleball led to a practical question: can a player understand why a point changed, then practice the right response? PINPOINT explores that connection between seeing, understanding and improving.

The concept links match capture and analysis to plain-language guidance and targeted training. The transferable principle is a useful learning loop: collect evidence, make it understandable and connect it to the next action.

Inside the work
  • Connects match capture, court calibration, movement analysis, plain-language explanations, and targeted training in one improvement loop.
  • Uses Court IQ scenarios to teach positioning, recovery, spacing, shot geometry, and the reason a point changed.
  • Builds Match Intelligence from player location, shot patterns, kitchen-line behavior, partner spacing, winners, errors, and pressure moments.
  • Develops a multidimensional player model across serve, return, dink, volley, positioning, reaction, consistency, power, accuracy, strategy, and awareness.
  • Extends the same intelligence layer into player profiles, equipment guidance, combine testing, and repeatable practice scenarios.

INTERACTIVE PROTOTYPE
The idea behind the work

A client should not need to manage the complexity of a production workflow to receive good work. This pipeline separates a simple intake and delivery experience from the stages the production team needs to run.

I structured the work around defined services, numbered stages and clear handoffs. The organization gains a way to track responsibility and progress, while the client gets a clearer experience of what happens next.

Inside the work
  • Runs every client job through ten numbered stages, 01 Intake to 10 Delivery, and the client only ever sees those two.
  • A 12-service rate card (research through media buying) prices work once; clients tick services at intake without seeing rates.
  • One board tracks many projects at once, each with per-stage progress bars, due-date countdowns, a project ID convention and an archive.
  • The video variant budgets each stage in time and money — about $675 of AI generation per finished video across script, stills, motion, sound and up-res.
  • A Google Apps Script backend writes one sheet row per project and proxies image generation into Drive, so API keys never reach the browser.

Unified Multi-Channel Point of Sale System

UNIT POS

INTERACTIVE PROTOTYPE
The idea behind the work

A café, a shop and a bar share the need to take an accurate order quickly, but their products and selling details differ. UNIT POS explores how one register can adapt to those different operating contexts.

I connected multiple ways of entering an item with catalogs and modifiers suited to each business type. The aim is practical consistency: a system that follows the work at the counter and gives the wider business usable transaction information.

Inside the work
  • One register serves three business types — café, retail, bar — switched from a single home-screen dropdown, each with its own catalog and modifier sets.
  • Items enter an order five ways: tap grid, name/SKU search, barcode scan, PLU keypad, and weight scale, sized for catalogs of hundreds of items.
  • Payment covers card, cash with change calculation, gift card and house account, splits up to 4 ways, and 4 tip presets.
  • The product database syncs from a Google Sheet, and tabs, refunds and end-of-day treat counter and web-store sales as one ledger.
  • One codebase renders both target devices, iPad (1194×834) and phone (390×844), with a persistent sync strip for Sheet, payments, web store, kitchen and offline status.
